Which Declining-Risk Pages Should an Editor Review First?

A leak-aware, client-held-out study on FlyRank lane data
Najam ul Hasan · FlyRank ML Internship capstone · October 2026
Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring · Claims are observed, directional and for decision-support only.
Abstract · 1 Problem · 2 Data · 3 Method · 4 Results · 5 Recommendations · 6 Limitations · 7 Reproducibility · 8 Acknowledgments

Abstract

Editors with limited time need to know which pages to review first when traffic is slipping. Using a pseudonymized FlyRank lane dataset of 10,550 pages from 29 clients, I defined a page as declining if its impressions in the last 30 days fell more than 20% against the previous 30 days, and tested whether features from earlier windows predict this on clients the model had never seen (leave-one-client-out). A transparent age-and-demand rule reached a mean per-client AUC of 0.561, and a gradient-boosted model reached 0.567, with no reliable difference between them (paired difference +0.006, 95% bootstrap interval −0.046 to +0.052). On the editor’s own objective, the share of a client’s lost impressions found in the top 20% of its pages, the classifier found 20.8% (random is 20.0%), while simply ranking by page impressions found 56.1%, and multiplying model risk by impressions added nothing (−0.5 points). The practical output is therefore a transparent triage list ranked by impressions at risk, with reason codes, and a clear statement that decline here is only weakly predictable from leak-free features.

1 Introduction and problem statement

Decision supported. An SEO editor has time to review a limited number of pages. Given a client’s pages, which should be reviewed first for a possible refresh? The unit of analysis is the page, the output is a ranked list with reason codes, and the cost of a wrong call is editor time spent on a page that was not actually losing traffic (or time not spent on one that was).

Question. Can a page’s later traffic decline be anticipated from its earlier impression history and static page descriptors, when evaluated on clients held out of training, and does any model add value over a transparent rule?

Why a baseline first. FlyRank’s own flags are hand-written rules with honest thresholds. A model is only worth deploying if it beats a rule an editor can read. This paper treats that comparison, not model accuracy in isolation, as the result.

2 Data

Source. FlyRank internship lane datasets (pseudonymized; June-2026 example cuts with 90-day windows), accessed under the FlyRank data-use terms. No client names, domains, URLs, queries or raw rows appear in this paper; clients are shown as letters.

2.2 Joining static page features

Page keys did not overlap with decline_recovery or ranking_lifecycle (0 shared pages and clients). engagement_fix shared 10,677 pages and all 33 clients, but its 30-day traffic columns equal this lane’s last window in 100% of matched pages, so they would leak the label and were not used. Only its static descriptors were taken: search intent, word-count tier and character-count tier (plus a freshness tier, used only as a sensitivity run because it is measured at the end of the label window).

2.3 Final analysis sample and exclusions

Pages need at least 100 impressions in the previous 30 days, giving 11,408 pages. I then kept only pages present in engagement_fix, leaving 10,550 pages from 29 clients (label rate 45.0%). The 858 excluded pages decline more (66.9% vs 45.0%) and decline faster (median last/previous impression ratio 0.59 vs 0.84). Because that lane is built from the last window, membership may itself depend on recent performance, so including “not in lane” as information would risk leakage. A sensitivity run that keeps them is reported in section 4.4. Only 10 of the 29 clients have at least 100 pages and are evaluated one by one.

3 Methodology

3.1 Label and assumptions

Label. A page is declining (1) if impressions_last_30d < 0.8 × impressions_prev_30d, among pages with at least 100 previous-window impressions. The 20% and 100-impression thresholds were fixed before looking at any model results.

Oldest window. The oldest 30-day window is derived as the 90-day total minus the two later windows, which assumes three exact, non-overlapping periods. Two development checks, run on an earlier 11,408-page version of the sample and not repeated in the final notebook, were consistent with this: older traffic correlates positively with later traffic (r = +0.40), and 57.8% of pages aged 31–90 days show no old-window traffic versus at most 0.2% in older tiers, as expected for pages too young to have had traffic then.

3.2 Features and exclusions

  • Used: log previous-window impressions, log oldest-window impressions, their difference (momentum), a no-old-traffic flag, page-age tier, and static descriptors (intent, word-count tier, character-count tier).
  • Left out on purpose: every 90-day column and last-30-day column (they contain the label window), trend_*, health_score, is_declining and all product flags, hashed identifiers (grouping only), and the engagement_fix traffic columns.

3.3 Baseline and models

Baseline rule. Score = 0.6 × (page age 181+ days) + 0.4 × percentile of previous-window impressions. It is transparent and was set before seeing results. Because this lane has page age but not “days since last update”, it is an adapted version of an earlier staleness rule, so it is not directly comparable with that earlier work.

Models. Gradient boosting (HistGradientBoostingClassifier, depth 3, learning rate 0.05, 150 iterations, seed 0) on four planned feature sets: age only; old-window features + age; + static descriptors (main model); + freshness (sensitivity only).

3.4 Validation

Leave-one-client-out. For each client, train on all other clients and score the held-out one, so no client’s pages appear in both train and test. The headline metric is the mean per-client AUC across the 10 clients with at least 100 pages, because decline rates differ widely across clients (about 17% to 57% among them) and a pooled AUC would partly measure which client a page belongs to. Differences are tested with a paired bootstrap over clients (5,000 resamples). Because the editor’s goal is impressions at stake, I also report the share of each client’s lost impressions found in its top 20% of pages.

4.2 The editor’s objective: lost impressions captured

AUC treats every page equally, but an editor with a review budget cares more about a 10,000-impression page than a 100-impression one. For each client I measured the share of its lost impressions found in the top 20% of its pages.

How to read this. Part of the 56% is built in: a 20% drop on a large page is more impressions than on a small one, so ranking by size captures most of the loss even with no skill at identifying which pages will fall. The informative comparison is expected loss vs impressions-only, where the model adds nothing. A persistence check also found only weak support for “earlier fall predicts later fall”: a later decline was more likely after an earlier fall in 6 of 10 clients (mean AUC of negative momentum 0.528).

4.4 Sensitivity and checks

  • Leak ablation (development run, not in the final notebook). Removing the derived old-window features lowered mean AUC from 0.558 to 0.518 in an earlier 11,408-page sample (+0.041, 95% interval +0.005 to +0.076 over 12 clients). Part of that gain likely reflects finer page-age information, since nearly all pages with no old-window traffic are in the youngest tier.
  • Keeping the 858 excluded pages raises the main model’s mean AUC from 0.567 to 0.596 but leaves the baseline roughly unchanged (0.561 to 0.552). I read this as a missing-feature/lane-membership effect, not real signal, and keep them out of the main analysis.
  • Static descriptors. Word and character counts looked strong when pooled (3,500+ words: 61% decline vs 38%), but that pattern came mostly from one client. Adding them gave a small, not statistically reliable gain (+0.016).
  • Reason codes. Two of four codes barely separate declining from non-declining pages (HIGH_DEMAND 46.1% vs 44.5%; NEGATIVE_MOMENTUM 46.6% vs 44.1%), while AGING_91_365 does better (47.7% vs 37.9%). Codes therefore explain why a page was flagged; they are not strong evidence of decline.

Operational result. In the page-level action list, a top-20%-per-client review budget (2,098 pages) held 56.4% of all lost impressions. REVIEW_FIRST pages (2,778) decline at 49.0% versus a 45.0% base rate yet hold 57.3% of lost impressions, again mostly because of their size. LOW_PRIORITY pages (779) decline at 24.9% and hold 2.7%.

How an editor would use it tomorrow. Within one client, sort pages by previous-window impressions, push down pages aged 31–90 days, review the largest pages in the 91–365 day tiers first, and treat reason codes as prompts for what to look at, not as evidence. Confidence: low to moderate for the ordering; low for any claim that a score identifies which individual page will fall.

6 Limitations and honest framing

  1. The model does not beat a simple rule, and is the wrong tool for the editor’s goal. Mean per-client AUC is 0.567 vs 0.561 (paired difference +0.006, 95% interval −0.046 to +0.052). On lost impressions captured, the classifier gets 20.8% (random 20.0%), the rule 50.9% and ranking by impressions 56.1%; adding model risk to impressions changes nothing (−0.5 points, interval −2.4 to +0.9).
  2. Weak where the value is. The client with 37.2% of pages and 46.9% of lost impressions has an AUC near 0.5 (0.507).
  3. One pair of windows. Leave-one-client-out tests transfer across clients, not across time. All windows come from a single June-2026 cut with no calendar dates, so seasonality or an algorithm update in the last window would hit every client at once.
  4. Pre-selected population and sample selection. The lane is a product-tagged subset, and 858 pages outside engagement_fix were dropped; they decline more (66.9% vs 45.0%) and lane membership may depend on recent activity.
  5. Regression to the mean and a broad decline. Pages are selected on at least 100 previous-window impressions and 45% are labelled declining, so part of the label is noise or a market-wide dip rather than page-specific decay.
  6. Small evaluation set. Only 10 of 29 clients have at least 100 pages; the smallest have few positive pages, so their AUCs are noisy and the bootstrap over 10 clients has little power.
  7. Concentrated losses. The top 1% of pages hold 19.8% of lost impressions and the top 10% hold 65.6%. One client holds 46.9% of lost impressions (37.2% of pages); the 365+ day high-demand segment’s share falls from 14.4% to 6.7% without it.
  8. Segments are in-sample and were defined after seeing the data. Thresholds are conventions, and some segments rest on 3 or 4 clients.
  9. Coarse and possibly late features. Word count, character count and age are tiers only and may be measured at the end of the label window. The derived oldest-window feature rests on an assumption about window alignment (section 3.1). Clicks, CTR and position cannot be used without leaking the label window.
  10. Impressions only. A fall in impressions can reflect reporting changes, seasonality or demand, not a content problem.
  11. Observational only. A high score means a page resembles pages that declined. No claim is made that refreshing a page changes its traffic, or anything about Google’s algorithm.
  12. Scores are not calibrated per client. They are trained on other clients, so they are risk scores for ranking within a client, not probabilities.
